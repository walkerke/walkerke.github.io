About the data

What this is: a demonstration of least-cost routing, not a proposed power line. Kendall and Fayetteville are existing substations on public record, picked because the ground between them is varied. The weights below are round numbers chosen to show the method. A real routing study weighs many more things, among them homes, land ownership, existing lines and roads, cultural sites and engineering limits.

The surface: 4,076,600 cells, each 100 meters on a side, covering about 15,740 square miles. A cell's cost is its land cover cost times a slope factor, times 5 on protected land, times 5 in critical habitat. Flat pasture or grassland is 1.

Closed land: protected areas managed mainly for biodiversity (GAP status 1 and 2 in the federal database) are closed to the route. That is about 250 square miles here. Other public and protected land is open at 5 times the cost.

The corridor: the path between the two substations with the lowest total cost, where each cell's cost counts for the distance crossed in it. It was found with route_corridor() in the spopt R package (version 0.1.3.9000), searching a grid in which each cell connects to 8 neighbors. The search took 0.4 seconds. The lighter lines are the next two alternatives from route_k_corridors(), which looks for routes that stay apart from the ones already found.

Sources: land cover is the Annual National Land Cover Database for 2024 (30 meter cells). Slope is from the U.S. Geological Survey's 3D Elevation Program 1 arc-second elevation. Protected areas are from the Protected Areas Database of the United States, version 4.1. Critical habitat is the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service's final designations. Substations are from the federal HIFLD substations file, April 2022.

What it can't tell you: whether a line could or should be built anywhere on this map. The surface leaves out most of what decides a real route, and its weights are not engineering costs. Costs are averaged over 100 meter cells, so a narrow river or road can vanish into its neighbors.

The cheapest path across a cost surface

Each cell is colored by how costly it would be to cross with a power line: cities, water, steep ground, protected land and critical habitat cost more. The blue line is the cheapest path between two existing substations: 145 miles against 132 in a straight line, at 34% less cost. It demonstrates a method and is not a proposed line.
